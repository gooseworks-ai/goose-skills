# Ideas — how a static ad gets an idea

An ad idea is **a real product fact or human situation, turned into one small physical event the viewer understands at a glance.** Lighting, type and colour make a good idea easy to read. They cannot rescue a missing one.

This file is the craft behind the concept-grid step. Read it whole before drafting.

## 1. The failure this prevents

Left alone, agents build ads from the feature list: product + headline + the product in use.

> A batch for an olive-oil brand came out as "Two oils. Different jobs." (two bottles), "Dinner. Finished." (oil drizzled on pasta), "Same bottle. Next chapter." (refill pour), "Cookies. Meet Frizzle." (bottle beside cookies), "Bring something for the cook." (gift box). Polished, accurate, and forgettable. Any olive oil could run four of them.

Every one of those is a **demonstration**: it shows the product doing its normal job. A demonstration is a valid ad when watching the action answers the buyer's real question. It is not an idea, and a batch of five demonstrations is one idea five times.

## 2. The method

### Step A: list starting points (10 or more)

A starting point is something true about the product or the people who use it. Write each one as a plain sentence with its source.

- **Product facts:** what it is, what's already done for you, what it leaves out, how it's made, how it's used. ("Tea and whole spices already blended." "Unsweetened.")
- **Human situations around the product:** the moment of use, who else is involved, what gets in the way, what it means to people. ("A parent has opinions about how you make chai." "Recipe research spirals." "It tastes like home to people who moved away.")

Situations come from customer words (reviews, the voice-of-customer bank, comments) when you have them. When you author one yourself, mark it **(hypothesis)**. A hypothesis can carry a scene. It cannot carry a factual claim.

### Step B: find the tension in each

For each starting point ask: **what does the person want, what gets in the way, and why does this product belong in that moment?** A starting point with no tension ("the oil is green") rarely makes an idea. Drop it or pair it with a situation.

### Step C: make the tension physical

Turn the tension into **one visible event**: something that could be built on a real set and photographed, even if it is impossible. Useful relationships:

| Relationship | Kolkata Chai example |
|---|---|
| Someone interferes | Mum's hand reaches out of the video call to point at the pot |
| Something gets in the way | Browser tabs become a paper maze around the saucepan |
| Something claims space | The cup sits inside a recessed calendar appointment |
| Two places connect | A miniature Kolkata balcony rises out of the saucepan |
| Someone gets invited, or left out | Spices sit at a tiny table; sugar gets the optional plus-one chair |
| A flat thing becomes real (or the reverse) | |
| Scale changes the meaning | |
| An object behaves like a person | |

The event must have a **reason**. Advice crosses the screen because a parent is getting involved. Tabs become a maze because research got confusing. Sugar becomes a guest because adding it is optional. A strange image with no reason is decoration.

### Step D: write the headline last

The headline **names the relationship**, not the effect, in words a person might say: "Mum has notes." "Sugar is a plus-one." "Sorry. Already booked." The image carries the joke. A short support line ties it to the product fact ("Unsweetened chai mix. Invite sugar if you like.").

### Step E: name the choice worth protecting

One sentence per ad: the detail that, if lost, kills the idea. "The hand must visibly cross the screen boundary; a normal video call loses the joke." "The sugar chair sits apart from the group." This sentence goes into the prompt word for word and is the first thing the reviewer checks.

## 3. Three tests before an idea is kept

1. **Swap test.** Replace our product with a competitor's. Does the ad still work? If yes, it is generic. Push it until the idea needs this product's fact or this brand's customer. ("Booked for chai" was the weakest Kolkata idea by this test: any drink brand could run it.)
2. **Thumbnail test.** Describe the image in one sentence a stranger understands. If you need two sentences, the scene is too busy.
3. **Truth test.** What would a buyer take away as fact? That takeaway must be a usable fact (`claims-and-policy.md` §2). A visibly imagined scene is not a claim that it happened; a promised result is.

## 4. The set

Five ads should be **five different meanings**, not five looks. Kolkata covered family, convenience, personal time, home and sweetness. Ask: if I cover the headlines, do two images say the same thing? Then merge them and find another starting point.

At most one ad in five may be a straight demonstration, and only when watching the action answers the buyer's main question.

## 5. Where references fit

A reference ad from another brand can give you a **device**, not a layout. The Oatside image of a carton coming out of a laptop gave permission for "an object crosses a screen". That device was used directly in one ad, loosely in two and not at all in two more, where the product fact carried the idea. See the find-references step for roles.

## 6. The worked example (do not copy)

| Starting point | Human situation | Physical event | Headline | Protect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Family involvement in making chai | A parent has advice, even from far away | Mum's hand reaches out of a video call toward the pot | Mum has notes. | Hand crosses the screen |
| Tea and spices already blended | Recipe research never ends | Browser tabs turn into a paper maze around the saucepan | Close the tabs. Put the chai on. | The tabs are physical paper |
| Chai as a daily ritual | Work fills every moment | A cup sits inside a recessed calendar appointment | Sorry. Already booked. | The cup is in the slot, not beside it |
| Chai and home | A routine carries home with it | A miniature Kolkata balcony rises from the saucepan | Home is a stovetop away. | The pot is the doorway |
| Unsweetened mix | You choose the sweetness | Spices at a tiny table; sugar on an extra chair | Sugar is a plus-one. | Sugar's chair sits apart |

These ideas belong to that brand. The method transfers; the jokes don't.
