# Creative taxonomy — what an ad is made of, and how to make ten that are truly different

**In short**
- Every static ad is one choice on each of **nine levers**: four about the *message* (persona, reason to buy, emotion, awareness), one about the *deal* (offer), and four about the *look* (framework, visual style, composition, type and colour).
- **A concept** is who it talks to, the one reason to buy and its proof. **An ad** is one execution of a concept: its framework, visual style, composition, type and hook. A good set has a few strong concepts, each in two or three clearly different looks. Two ads that differ only in their words are **one ad** to Meta.
- Meta's delivery system (Andromeda, 2025) groups ads that look or open alike and lets only one of them compete. A set is diverse only when its ads differ in **message and look at the same time**.
- Use the grid in §3 before composing any ad, and the diversity checks and the **concept gate** in §4 before showing the plan. Difference is not enough — every concept must also be good.

The angle families, awareness stages and hook formulas live in `angles-and-hooks.md`; the archetype table and company types live in `formats-and-placements.md` §1–2. This file is the frame that ties them together.

## 1. What "different concepts" means — AG1's live ads (US Ad Library, 19 Oct 2026)

AG1 is one product family with one brand look, yet its top static ads each sell a different reason, to a different person, in a different look:

| # | What the image says and shows | Reason to buy | Persona | Emotion | Framework | Visual style |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Kinda chic that I don't have to remember to take creatine every day (bc it's already in my AG1 Pro)" — a woman drinking it in her car | One less thing to remember | Busy woman who trains | Relief, a little humour | UGC-style text over photo | Lo-fi selfie |
| 2 | "The list of things I recommend is pretty short, but AG1 is absolutely on it." — a doctor, named, holding the bottle | An expert you trust vouches for it | Health-curious sceptic | Trust | Expert endorsement quote | Hi-fi portrait |
| 3 | No words — an athlete tearing open a stick pack in a dark studio | It belongs in a serious training routine | Athlete | Aspiration | Product in use | Hi-fi studio |
| 4 | "STRONGER mornings start here." with callouts: "5g creatine monohydrate", "Ca-HMB" | What's inside and what each part does | Performance-minded buyer comparing stacks | Confidence | Feature callouts | Designed graphic + photo |
| 5 | "Tastes like apple and strawberry. Works like part of my routine." — gummies in a palm, pouch beside them | It tastes good and fits a busy day | Busy adult who hates powders | Ease, pleasure | Caption card over in-hand photo | Lo-fi native |
| 6 | "Your Welcome Kit awaits you" — opened box, list of free items (shaker, canister, scoop) | You get extra things free when you start | New customer on the fence | Excitement | Offer / unboxing flat lay | Polished product |
| 7 | "By AG1 having NSF certification, you know it's safe, effective, and quality-sourced." — a known athlete drinking it in a gym | It's certified clean and safe | Athlete worried about what's in supplements | Reassurance | Testimonial + certification proof | Hi-fi lifestyle |

Its ads that week also included a military discount, a "sold out, it's back" scarcity push, a celebrity morning routine and a new sleep product.

**The lesson:** no two rows share a reason to buy, and few share a persona, emotion or look. That is concept diversity — across concepts. Inside each concept, a brand like AG1 then runs two or three looks (it runs several versions of most of these); this playbook does the same (§3). The failure it guards against: ten ads that all say "the drink that helps your health" on ten backgrounds. (AG1's real claims are their own; a brand using this playbook may only say what its own brain allows — `claims-and-policy.md` §2.)

## 2. The nine levers

### Message levers (what the ad says, and to whom)

| Lever | The question | Values to rotate | Where the answer comes from |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Persona** | Who exactly is this ad talking to? | The brand's real segments: e.g. new parent, gym regular, busy founder, gift-giver, sceptic who tried everything, commercial vs residential buyer | Audience research, voice-of-customer segments, the brand's own ads and comments |
| **2. Reason to buy (angle)** | Why would *this* person buy *now*? | The 12 angle families in `angles-and-hooks.md` §3 | Angle bank, customer words, usable brain facts, the brand's running ads |
| **3. Emotion** | What should they feel in the first second? | Relief, pride, curiosity, fear of missing out, belonging, aspiration, humour, guilt-then-relief, calm, excitement. Never fear or shame about the body (policy) | Fit to the reason to buy; the brand's voice rules |
| **4. Awareness** | What do they already know? | Unaware · problem-aware · solution-aware · product-aware · most-aware (`angles-and-hooks.md` §2) | The brief (cold vs retargeting) |

**Hook** sits inside a reason to buy: several hooks can carry one angle ("NEW cozy boots you won't want to take off" vs "I wore these all day and forgot to take them off" — same angle, two hooks). Hooks are the cheapest variable; they are not concept diversity.

### Deal lever

| Lever | The question | Values | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| **5. Offer** | What do they get for acting now? | None (evergreen) · % off · bundle / gift with purchase · free shipping · subscription perk · identity discount (military, students) · new launch · back in stock | Only a live offer the brand gave, with its terms and end date. Never invented. Different offers pull different buyers (a 70%-off sale draws price hunters; buy-1-get-2 draws heavy users) |

### Look levers (how the ad looks)

| Lever | The question | Values to rotate |
|---|---|---|
| **6. Framework (archetype)** | What shape is the ad? | Product hero · benefit stack / listicle · us vs them / old way vs new · review or testimonial card · problem → agitate → solution · offer / promo · stat or proof card · press quote · founder note · notes-app / tweet / text-message native · meme · flat lay / unboxing · feature callouts · educational / myth-buster · news / "new" announcement · before/after (gated) — table in `formats-and-placements.md` §1 |
| **7. Visual style** | How polished does it look? | **Lo-fi native** (phone photo, raw, looks like a post) · **Hi-fi polished** (studio, editorial, premium) · **Designed graphic** (flat colour, bold type, no photo) · **Illustrated / AI-surreal** (openly stylised, never a fake person posing as a customer) · **Lifestyle** (product in a real moment of use) |
| **8. Composition** | Where do the eye and the product sit? | Centred product · product off-centre with big type · split screen · grid / collage · close-up texture · in-hand · in-scene (on a desk, in a gym bag) · text-only · screenshot frame · top-down flat lay |
| **9. Type and colour** | What does the text look like? | Brand palette vs a contrast palette · one bold display face vs a serif editorial · handwritten · system UI font (for native frames) · type size and weight · light vs dark background |

**Media type** (static, video, carousel) is a tenth lever that sits above this playbook: statics reach people who mostly engage with images, so a brand running only video should add statics, and vice versa.

## 3. The concept grid — fill it before composing any ad

One row per ad. The agent fills every column; "same as #N" is allowed only for the one variable that cell is testing.

| # | Concept | Persona | Reason to buy (they buy because…) | Proof | Emotion | Awareness | Offer | Framework | Visual style | Composition | Type & colour | Hook | Source of the reason |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**Shape of a good set: a few strong concepts, each in more than one look.** Ten different mediocre ads lose to three or four strong concepts with two or three executions each — and that shape makes test results readable (the concept wins or loses as a unit). Default for ten ads: **3–5 concepts × 2–3 executions** (executions differ in framework, style or composition, never only in words).

**Two hard rules (always):**
1. **No two ads share reason to buy + framework + visual style.** Two executions of one concept must differ in look; two concepts must differ in reason to buy.
2. **The cover-the-text test passes** (§4.1).

**Spread targets — scale them to what the brand really has, never invent to hit them:**
- **Reasons to buy:** as many grounded ones as the brand has, up to one per concept. A one-product brand with two true reasons ships two concepts done well, and the plan says so. A padded reason is the "off-message" failure this playbook exists to fix.
- **Personas:** every real segment the brand sells to; one is fine for a single-segment brand (say so).
- **Frameworks and visual styles:** the easiest spread to add — aim for at least three of each across ten ads, no single one on more than ~40% of the set.
- **Awareness: match the brief, don't spread it.** Cold prospecting sets sit in unaware → solution-aware; offer-forward and most-aware ads belong in retargeting sets. Spread stages only when the brief covers both.
- **Emotion is a description, not a diversity lever.** Two ads labelled "curiosity" and "aspiration" that look alike are still alike. Count emotion only when the picture changes with it.
- **Offer ads** only with a live offer; at most two in ten unless the brief is a sale.
- Every row names where its reason came from (angle bank id, customer-words row, brain fact with its status, the brand's own running ad, the marketer in this chat). A reason with no source is not used.

**Iterate / rotation sets** change one named lever per ad from the winner and keep the rest: a new hook (cheapest), a new framework or style (new look, same message), or a new reason to buy (when the angle is tired).

**Talk to the marketer in concepts, not levers.** The grid is the agent's working sheet. The plan shows each concept as one plain sentence — "For busy parents: back to sleep in minutes — proof: 4,000 reviews — shown as a handwritten note and as a product hero" — and keeps the lever columns in the batch file.

## 4. Checks before showing the plan

**Diversity:**
1. **The "same ad" test.** Cover the text on each image. If two ads look alike with the words hidden, they are one ad to Meta: change the framework, style or composition of one. (Text overlays alone never make two ads different.)
2. **The "they buy because" test.** Read each concept's reason aloud. If two say the same thing in other words, merge them — into one concept with two executions, not two concepts.
3. **The persona test.** Could the ad be shown to a different persona unchanged? Then it isn't speaking to anyone. Name the person in the image, the words or the situation.
4. **The tally.** Count reasons, personas, frameworks and styles. A gap the brand can fill is filled; a gap it can't fill is named in one line ("one persona only", "no offer live").

**Quality — the concept gate (before any money is spent).** Score each concept pass/fail on five checks; a concept that fails one is fixed or dropped, never rendered:

| Check | Passes when |
|---|---|
| One specific, believable promise | The reason to buy is concrete ("back to sleep in minutes", not "better sleep") and backed by a usable brain fact or a plain product fact |
| A proof element | A real rating or review count, a specific number, a guarantee, a press quote, a demo, the founder's story — or a framework that shows the product working. No proof on file → say so, never invent one |
| Hook reads at thumbnail size | The on-image hook is ≤ 8 words and makes sense in under a second at 300 px wide (a confirmed claim worded as recorded, or a real quote, may run longer only if it still reads in two lines at phone size) |
| Message lands in three seconds | A stranger would get who it's for and why, from the image alone |
| Persona fit | The situation, words and scene belong to that persona's life, ideally in their own words |

**Feasibility.** Market research leads, frameworks follow. Don't force a framework because it is on the list. A "founder note" needs the founder's real words; a "review card" needs a real review; a "before/after" needs the platform and category to allow it. Missing input → pick another framework.
