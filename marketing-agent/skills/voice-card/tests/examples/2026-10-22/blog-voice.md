# Papertrail — company blog voice guide, 22 October 2026

**Status: provisional.** This guide uses five complete but very short fictional company articles: 898 body words in total. The owner has not confirmed it. It describes this sample; it does not replace the confirmed brand rules or show how long real full-length articles should be.

## What the sample says

The writing starts with a specific invoice-review situation, then gives the reader a small thing to check. It treats the reader as a capable teammate. It keeps review and approval separate and avoids treating a tidy record as proof that a decision was right.

## Measured mechanics

- Five articles: median **180 body words**, range 177–182. This narrow length reflects the short fixture, not a rule for real long-form content.
- 85 sentences: median **11 words**, range 4–18. Practical draft target from this sample: one thought per sentence, with longer sentences when the explanation needs them.
- 25 paragraphs including CTAs: median **42 words**, range 4–48. Excluding CTA-only paragraphs: median 43, range 36–48.
- Three sentence-case action subheadings in every article; no numbered or bullet lists in the bodies.
- Five final CTAs, four to eight words, beginning “See”. No mid-article CTA, emoji, exclamation mark or em dash in these supplied bodies.
- The sample mainly uses “you/your” or the team, with no first-person founder story. Company terms such as invoice, review, approval and export are explained by their role in the task.

## Five rules a writer can check

| Rule | On-voice evidence | Plausible off-voice version |
|---|---|---|
| Begin with the concrete situation before giving advice | “An invoice arrives with a price nobody recognises.” — article 1 | “Invoice management requires a comprehensive strategic approach.” |
| Ask for the smallest decision the next person needs | “A handover note should make that question visible before it tells the next person what to do.” — article 3 | “Your handover should contain every detail of the entire process.” |
| Explain a record's limit as plainly as its use | “The file is a record, not independent proof that the decision was right.” — article 2 | “A professional export demonstrates that the process is correct.” |
| Keep review and approval distinct | “A review can explain why an invoice looks wrong. An approval decides whether the payment should move forward.” — article 4 | “Once the review is complete, the payment is approved.” |
| End with a check the reader could actually perform | “Compare a few similar exceptions. Name what they share and what differs.” — article 5 | “Transform your entire finance workflow for the future.” |

The examples are style evidence, not independent proof or approval of a commercial promise. A neutral explanation of review/approval is safe to quote here; no result is invented.

## Article pattern

Open with one recognisable situation in roughly a short paragraph. Use three action headings to explain what to keep together, what remains unknown and what to check next. Finish with a small next step, then one literal “See…” CTA. Transitions connect the same question across paragraphs; they do not restart the article with a slogan.

## Terminology and suggested never-says

Use “review” for the finding/question and “approval” for the payment authority. Keep “invoice exception”, “reviewer decision”, “record” and “export” concrete. The supplied brand rules require plain language and human approval, and forbid unproved time-saved claims.

No superlatives or automation promises appear in this corpus. Their absence is a style observation, not a newly confirmed prohibited-word list. Do not turn this provisional guide into an instruction to use 180-word articles everywhere. Social writers borrow the vocabulary and tone, then use their channel's own length/opening rules.

## Three rewrites

1. Generic: “Our revolutionary platform transforms invoice management.” → “Review invoice exceptions in a shared queue.” This uses an approved product line from the supplied brand context.
2. Generic: “Comprehensive documentation unlocks unrivalled operational confidence.” → “Keep the question and the reviewer decision together.” This describes a neutral writing topic, not a product result.
3. Generic: “Seamlessly revolutionise your approval process.” → “Keep your existing approval steps.” This uses the supplied approved product line and keeps the product boundary intact.
